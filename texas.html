Texas: Everything You Need to Know

Bigger than most countries, with an economy to match. This is the complete guide — the history, the four cities that run the state, the oil and the tech, the food nobody argues about, and the land that goes from beach to desert without ever leaving state lines.

~30 MIN READ·UNITED STATES·ARTICLE 12

Texas is the only state in the U.S. that was ever its own independent country — twice, if you count the brief period as part of Mexico separately from its decade as the Republic of Texas. That history isn't a trivia footnote; it's the reason Texans talk about their state the way other people talk about their nation, complete with its own flag flown at equal height to the American flag, its own folklore, and a place in the American imagination that no other state quite occupies.

This guide is built the same way we build every deep-dive: no tourist-brochure fluff, real numbers, and enough detail that it holds up whether you've never set foot in the state or you've lived in Houston your whole life. We go through the land, the history, the cities, the economy, the food, and the culture — and at the end, we separate what's actually true about Texas from what's just a very good story.

The Basics: Size, Scale & Population

Texas is the second-largest U.S. state by both area and population, but the numbers undersell how genuinely enormous it is. At roughly 695,000 square kilometers, Texas is bigger than France, and if it were an independent country today, its economy alone would rank among the largest in the world — bigger than Canada's or Russia's by most measures of gross state product.

That last fact surprises people: Texas is wide enough to span two time zones. Driving from the eastern border with Louisiana to the western tip near El Paso takes longer than driving from Chicago to New York — and El Paso is, famously, closer to San Diego, California than it is to Dallas.

How Texas Became Texas

Long before Texas was Texas, it was home to dozens of Indigenous nations — the Caddo, Comanche, Apache, and Karankawa among them — whose presence shaped the land for thousands of years before European contact. Spanish explorers arrived in the 1500s, and Texas spent nearly three centuries as a remote, thinly populated edge of the Spanish Empire, then briefly of independent Mexico after 1821.

The Texas State Capitol in Austin — its dome stands taller than the U.S. Capitol in Washington, D.C.

The Republic era

Mexico actively encouraged American settlers into Texas in the 1820s to help populate the territory, a decision that backfired within a generation. Cultural and political tensions between the growing Anglo-American population and the Mexican government escalated into the Texas Revolution in 1835–36. The war is remembered above all for the Battle of the Alamo, where a small garrison in San Antonio held out for 13 days against a much larger Mexican force before being overrun — a defeat that became the rallying cry "Remember the Alamo" and helped galvanize the decisive Texan victory weeks later at the Battle of San Jacinto.

Texas then existed as a fully independent nation — the Republic of Texas — for nearly a decade, complete with its own president, currency, and foreign embassies, before voluntarily joining the United States in 1845 as the 28th state. That decade of independence is the direct reason the state flag flies at equal height to the U.S. flag on Texas state property, a privilege afforded to no other state.

The U.S. and Texas flags — flown at equal height, by law

Since statehood

Texas joined the Confederacy during the Civil War and was placed under military reconstruction afterward. The most consequential turning point in its modern history came in 1901, when the Spindletop oil well near Beaumont blew and revealed the scale of oil beneath Texas soil — an event that redirected the entire trajectory of the state's economy for the next century and is discussed in more detail below.

03 / 08

The Big Four Cities

Texas doesn't have one dominant city the way many states or countries do — it has four major metropolitan areas, each with a genuinely different identity, and together they hold the overwhelming majority of the state's population.

Downtown Dallas by day

Houston — the energy capital

Houston is the largest city in Texas and the fourth-largest in the United States. It's the undisputed global capital of the energy industry, home to NASA's Johnson Space Center (mission control for crewed spaceflight since the Gemini program), and one of the most ethnically diverse major cities in the entire country — more languages are spoken in Houston's school district than in almost any other in the U.S.

Houston's skyline at dusk, seen from Buffalo Bayou Park

Dallas–Fort Worth — the business hub

Technically two cities that have grown into one enormous metroplex, Dallas–Fort Worth is a finance, banking, and corporate headquarters hub — more Fortune 500 companies are headquartered here than in almost any other U.S. metro area. Dallas is also home to some genuinely striking modern architecture, like the Margaret Hunt Hill Bridge, whose single arch has become an unofficial symbol of the city's skyline.

Austin — the capital and the outlier

Austin is the state capital and, culturally, the odd one out — a famously liberal-leaning, music-obsessed college town (home to the University of Texas's flagship campus) inside an otherwise more conservative state, summed up by its own unofficial motto: "Keep Austin Weird." It's also become one of the fastest-growing tech hubs in the country, drawing major operations from companies like Tesla and Apple.

San Antonio — the historic heart

San Antonio is the second-largest city in Texas and arguably its most historic, home to the Alamo itself and the picturesque River Walk that winds through downtown. It has the largest concentration of Spanish colonial missions in the U.S., five of which make up a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

The Economy: Oil, Tech & Everything Between

If Texas were an independent country, its GDP would rank among the top ten to fifteen economies in the world, ahead of nations like Canada, South Korea, or Russia depending on the year. That scale comes from genuine diversification, not just one industry.

TOP INDUSTRIES
Energy, tech, aerospace, agriculture, healthcare
STATE INCOME TAX
None (funded via sales & property tax)
MAJOR TECH HUB
Austin ("Silicon Hills")
ENERGY CAPITAL
Houston

Oil built the modern Texas economy, and it's still central — Texas produces more crude oil than any other U.S. state by a wide margin, and would rank among the world's top oil-producing nations on its own. But the state's zero personal income tax policy has also made it a magnet for corporate relocations and tech expansion over the last two decades, with companies like Tesla, Oracle, and HP moving significant operations or full headquarters to Texas. Aerospace is a major pillar too, anchored by NASA in Houston and a growing private space industry around companies like SpaceX's launch operations in South Texas.

Food Culture: BBQ, Tex-Mex & State Pride on a Plate

Texans take barbecue seriously enough that it functions almost as a regional religion, with genuine, heated debate over technique between different parts of the state.

Central Texas-style smoked brisket, sliced

Central Texas BBQ

The style most associated with Texas today: beef brisket, slow-smoked over post oak wood for as long as 12–18 hours, seasoned simply with salt and pepper rather than a heavy sauce, and traditionally served on butcher paper rather than a plate. Towns like Lockhart, near Austin, are treated as genuine pilgrimage destinations for BBQ enthusiasts.

Tex-Mex

Distinct from both traditional Mexican cuisine and American food, Tex-Mex emerged from over a century of overlapping Texan and Mexican culinary tradition along the border region. Dishes like the crispy beef taco, chili con carne, and the fully melted-cheese enchilada plate are Tex-Mex inventions that many people outside Texas mistake for standard Mexican cooking.

Beyond these two pillars, Texas food culture includes serious chili traditions (a bowl of red, famously made without beans in competition-style Texas chili), kolaches (a Czech pastry brought by 19th-century immigrants and now a beloved Texas breakfast staple, especially around Central Texas), and a Gulf Coast seafood tradition running along the entire coastline.

06 / 08

The Land: Gulf Coast to Big Bend

Few U.S. states contain this much genuine ecological range. Texas spans four distinct climate regions and terrains, and you can drive through nearly all of them without ever crossing a state line.

A Gulf Coast fishing pier at sunset near Port Aransas

The Gulf Coast

Texas has over 590 kilometers of Gulf of Mexico coastline, home to barrier islands like Padre Island — the longest undeveloped barrier island in the world — as well as major shipping ports, fishing towns, and beach communities that feel worlds apart from the state's inland image.

Hill Country & the High Plains

Central Texas gives way to the Hill Country — rolling limestone hills, spring-fed rivers, and vineyard country around Fredericksburg — while the Panhandle in the north sits on the flat High Plains, part of the same Great Plains system that stretches up into Kansas and Nebraska.

Big Bend & the desert West

Far West Texas is a different world again: the Chihuahuan Desert, the Davis Mountains (home to some of the darkest night skies and best stargazing conditions in North America), and Big Bend National Park, where the Rio Grande carves through canyons over 460 meters deep along the Mexican border.

07 / 08

Culture, Sports & the Texas Identity

Texas pride is not a stereotype exaggerated for outsiders — it's a genuinely distinct regional identity, reinforced constantly through education (Texas history is a required, dedicated subject in the state's schools), symbolism, and sport.

Downtown Houston at night

Football, rodeo & Friday nights

High school football in Texas is a genuine civic institution, not just a school sport — some Texas high schools have built stadiums seating 10,000 or more spectators, and the phrase "Friday night lights" (which became a book, then a film, then a long-running TV series) describes a real weekly ritual across small-town Texas. Rodeo remains a living tradition too, not a museum piece: the Houston Livestock Show and Rodeo is one of the largest rodeo events in the world, drawing over two million visitors annually.

"Don't Mess With Texas"

One of the most successful slogans in American advertising history started life in 1986 as an anti-littering campaign for the Texas Department of Transportation — and was adopted so completely by the public that it became a de facto statement of statewide identity, printed on everything from license plates to t-shirts, almost entirely disconnected from its original highway-litter meaning.

08 / 08

Myths vs. Reality

Texas carries more myth and exaggeration than almost any other U.S. state — some of it true, some of it very much not.

  • MYTH

    Texas can legally secede from the United States whenever it wants, or split into five states at will.

    Reality: Neither is true today. The 1845 annexation resolution once discussed a future division into multiple states, but the U.S. Supreme Court ruled unilateral secession unconstitutional in 1869 (Texas v. White), and any state split would still require Congressional approval.

  • MYTH

    Everything really is bigger in Texas.

    Reality: Often genuinely true by the numbers — Texas leads the U.S. in land area among the lower 48, cattle production, and wind energy generation — but Alaska is larger, and several states beat Texas in specific categories like population density or ski terrain.

  • MYTH

    It's all desert and cowboys.

    Reality: As covered above, Texas has beach coastline, pine forests in the east, sub-tropical wetlands near Houston, and some of the most ethnically diverse, densely urban cities in the entire country.

Texas spent a decade as its own country, keeps its flag flying at the same height as the American one, and still argues about barbecue sauce like it's a matter of state security. That combination — real history, real scale, and real pride — is why no other state quite compares.
