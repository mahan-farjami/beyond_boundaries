Golf: The Complete Guide

Every part of the game in one place — where it came from, how the rules actually work, what's in the bag and why, how a course is built, how scoring really works, the swing fundamentals, the four majors, the etiquette nobody explains, and exactly how to get started if you've never picked up a club.

~35 MIN READ·GLOBAL SPORT·ARTICLE 15

Golf has a reputation problem before people even try it: it looks slow, the rules seem needlessly complicated, and the terminology sounds like a private club invented it specifically to keep outsiders out. None of that reputation is really deserved. At its core, golf is one of the simplest games ever invented — hit a ball into a hole using the fewest strokes possible — and almost everything else is detail that becomes intuitive within a few rounds.

This guide covers that detail properly, in order, from history through to your actual first swing. Nothing is assumed. If you finish this and go stand on a tee box for the first time, you'll understand more about what's happening than most people do after a full season of playing.

What Golf Actually Is

Every hole starts the same way: a ball on a tee, and a plan

The entire objective of golf, stripped of all jargon: get a small ball from a starting point (the tee) into a hole, hundreds of yards away, using as few strokes as possible. A full round is typically 18 holes, played in sequence, with your total strokes across all 18 added up to determine your final score. Lower is always better — this is the one part of golf that trips up total beginners, since in almost every other sport, more points is the goal.

STANDARD ROUND
18 holes
TYPICAL ROUND TIME
4–5 hours
GOAL
Lowest total strokes wins
TYPICAL COURSE LENGTH
6,000–7,200+ yards

A Brief History of Golf

Golf's most widely accepted origin traces to 15th-century Scotland, where a stick-and-ball game played over open linksland (the sandy, coastal terrain between beach and farmland) evolved into something close to the modern game. King James II of Scotland actually banned golf in 1457, worried it was distracting men from archery practice needed for national defense — an early sign of just how addictive the game already was.

Formalization

The Honourable Company of Edinburgh Golfers wrote down the first formal rules of golf in 1744, and the Old Course at St Andrews — still played today and widely regarded as "the Home of Golf" — became the sport's spiritual center. The now-standard 18-hole round became the norm largely because St Andrews had 18 holes, and other courses followed its example rather than any deliberate design logic.

Golf goes global

Scottish and English emigrants carried golf across the British Empire and to the United States through the 19th century. The sport's modern global professional structure — the major championships, world rankings, and televised tours — largely took shape through the 20th century, with the game's popularity expanding enormously alongside television coverage from the 1950s onward.

The Equipment: What's in the Bag

The basics: a driver, glove, cap, and balls — everything a new player actually needs to start

Rules permit up to 14 clubs in a bag during a round, though beginners need nowhere near that many to start playing and learning.

Driver

The longest club, used for maximum distance off the tee on longer holes. Least forgiving of mishits.

Fairway Woods

Shorter than a driver, used for long shots from the fairway or a shorter tee shot needing accuracy over raw distance.

Irons (numbered)

The workhorse clubs, numbered roughly 3 through 9 — lower numbers hit the ball farther and lower; higher numbers hit shorter and higher.

Wedges

Specialized short irons for close-range, high-precision shots — approach shots, bunkers, and shots requiring a steep, soft landing.

Putter

Used exclusively on the green for rolling the ball into the hole — the only club with no loft designed to lift the ball into the air.

The ball

Modern golf balls have dimples (typically 300–500 of them) that reduce air drag and increase lift compared to a smooth ball, allowing significantly greater distance — a smooth ball of identical weight and size would travel roughly half as far when struck the same way.

What beginners actually need

A basic beginner set (driver, a few irons, a wedge, and a putter), a glove for grip and blister prevention, and appropriate shoes is genuinely sufficient to start. Most golf facilities also rent clubs, making the actual barrier to trying golf for the first time far lower than the sport's expensive reputation suggests.

The Rules That Actually Matter

The official Rules of Golf run to hundreds of pages, but the vast majority of casual and even serious amateur play only ever touches a small, practical subset.

Play the ball as it lies

The foundational principle of golf: you generally must play the ball from wherever it comes to rest, without improving its position, unless a specific rule allows relief (moving the ball, usually with a one-stroke penalty).

Out of bounds & lost balls

If a ball goes out of bounds or is lost, the standard procedure is "stroke and distance" — replay the shot from where you last hit it, adding one penalty stroke, meaning a lost tee shot typically costs two total strokes (the penalty plus the shot you're replaying).

Hazards: water and bunkers

A ball in a water hazard usually allows a one-stroke penalty drop at a specified point rather than requiring you to play from the water itself. Bunkers (sand traps) must be played as they lie — you can't ground your club in the sand before your swing, one of the rules beginners break most often without realizing it.

The flagstick

A 2019 rule change now allows the flagstick to remain in the hole while putting, removing a long-standing requirement to remove it first — a small but widely welcomed simplification.

Course Anatomy

Every golf hole is built from the same basic zones, in the same order, regardless of the course.

ZONEWHAT IT IS
Tee boxThe starting area for the hole; multiple tee positions exist for different skill levels
FairwayThe closely mown, primary playing corridor between tee and green
RoughLonger grass bordering the fairway, penalizing inaccurate shots with a harder next shot
HazardsBunkers (sand) and water features, positioned to add strategic risk
GreenThe extremely short, precisely maintained putting surface surrounding the hole
Pin/FlagstickMarks the exact hole location on the green, which moves daily

Par, explained

Every hole has a "par" — the number of strokes a skilled player is expected to need. Par 3 holes are short (typically reachable from the tee), Par 4 holes are mid-length (needing a tee shot plus an approach), and Par 5 holes are long (generally requiring three shots to reach the green). A standard 18-hole course usually totals to par 70–72 across the full round.

How Scoring Really Works

Golf scoring terms sound intimidating but all measure the same simple thing: strokes taken relative to par on that hole.

TERMMEANING
Eagle2 strokes under par on a hole
Birdie1 stroke under par
ParExactly the expected number of strokes
Bogey1 stroke over par
Double bogey2 strokes over par
Hole-in-oneThe ball goes in on the very first stroke, from the tee

The handicap system

A handicap is a number representing a player's skill level relative to a standardized course rating, allowing players of very different abilities to compete fairly against each other by adjusting their effective score. A beginner might carry a handicap of 30 or higher; skilled amateurs often play in the single digits; touring professionals typically play at or below a "scratch" (zero) handicap.

The Swing: Fundamentals

Address position — where every good swing actually begins

The golf swing looks complex from the outside, but nearly every beginner mistake traces back to one of a handful of fundamentals.

Grip

The connection between hands and club is the single most influential factor in shot direction. A grip that's too tight restricts the natural wrist action needed for clubhead speed; the most common beginner error is gripping far harder than necessary, tensing the whole arm in the process.

Stance and posture

A balanced, athletic stance — knees slightly flexed, weight centered, spine tilted forward from the hips rather than hunched from the shoulders — creates the base every consistent swing is built on.

The swing path

At its simplest, the golf swing is a rotational movement, not an arm-only motion — power comes primarily from hip and torso rotation, with the arms and club following that rotation rather than driving it independently. This single reframing (rotate, don't just swing your arms) is often the fastest single improvement a total beginner can make.

Follow-through

A complete, balanced follow-through, finishing with weight fully shifted onto the front foot, is a reliable indicator of a well-executed swing — rushed, incomplete follow-throughs are strongly associated with mishit shots.

The Four Majors & Legendary Players

Professional golf has four tournaments universally recognized as "majors" — the most prestigious events in the sport, and the primary measure by which a professional career is judged.

MAJORDETAIL
The MastersHeld every April at Augusta National, Georgia — the only major played at the same course every year
PGA ChampionshipOrganized by the PGA of America, held in May
U.S. OpenRun by the USGA, known for extremely difficult course setups and thick rough
The Open ChampionshipThe oldest major (first played in 1860), held in the UK on links-style courses, often called simply "The Open"

Legendary names worth knowing

Jack Nicklaus holds the record for most men's major championships (18) across a career spanning the 1960s–1980s. Tiger Woods redefined the sport's global popularity and athleticism starting in the late 1990s, with 15 major titles of his own. On the women's side, Annika Sörenstam and more recently a deep field of major champions have driven the growth of the LPGA Tour into a genuinely global, highly competitive circuit.

Etiquette Nobody Explains

Golf etiquette isn't just tradition for its own sake — most of it exists for genuinely practical reasons: pace of play, course preservation, and safety.

Pace of play

Slow play is widely considered the single biggest etiquette failure in golf. Be ready to hit when it's your turn, and if your group falls behind the group ahead, it's standard practice to let faster groups play through.

Repair what you disturb

Replace or fill divots (the turf displaced by an iron shot), rake bunkers smooth after your shot, and repair ball marks on the green (a small indentation left when a ball lands) — all standard, expected practice that keeps the course playable for everyone after you.

Quiet and stillness during a shot

Stand still and stay silent while another player is addressing or hitting their ball — a simple courtesy rooted in the fact that golf, unlike most team sports, requires precise concentration in a shared, unstructured environment.

Safety first

Never swing a club or hit a shot when anyone is within range ahead of you, and shout "Fore!" immediately and loudly if a shot is heading toward another person or group — a genuine safety practice, not just tradition.

How to Actually Get Started

A bucket of range balls — the cheapest, lowest-pressure way to start

The single most common mistake new golfers make is going straight to a full 18-hole course before ever hitting a ball. There's a much lower-friction path in.

Start at the driving range

A bucket of range balls costs a small fraction of a round of golf and lets you practice the actual swing mechanics with zero pressure, no one waiting behind you, and no course rules to worry about yet.

Take at least a few lessons

A handful of lessons from a certified instructor early on will save months of practicing incorrect habits that become genuinely hard to unlearn later — widely considered the single best investment a new golfer can make, more valuable than any equipment purchase.

Play a short or executive course first

Shorter 9-hole or "executive" courses (made up mostly of par-3s) let new players experience real on-course play, including etiquette and pace, without the multi-hour commitment and difficulty of a full 18-hole championship layout.

Myths vs. Reality

Golf balls on a practice green — the game is more accessible than its reputation suggests
  • MYTH

    Golf is only for wealthy, older people.

    Reality: Public courses, driving ranges, and second-hand equipment make entry-level golf genuinely affordable in most regions, and the sport's average player age has been trending younger for years, partly driven by simulator venues and shorter course formats.

  • MYTH

    Golf isn't real exercise.

    Reality: Walking an 18-hole course (without a cart) typically covers 6–12 kilometers, and the swing itself is a genuine full-body rotational movement — studies on golfers' fitness consistently push back on the "not a real sport" stereotype.

  • MYTH

    You need to be good before you can enjoy it.

    Reality: The handicap system exists specifically so players of very different skill levels can compete fairly and enjoy the same round together — genuine enjoyment doesn't require genuine skill in golf any more than it does in any other hobby.

  • MYTH

    It's a slow, boring game to watch.

    Reality: A single major championship Sunday routinely draws tens of millions of global viewers — the tension of a close leaderboard on the back nine is widely considered some of the most gripping drama in sport, precisely because of, not despite, the pace.

Every rule, every piece of equipment, and every strange tradition in golf exists to answer one question: how do you fairly measure a shot that depends entirely on precision, patience, and terrain that's never quite the same twice? That's the whole game.
