SURVIVAL GUIDES — VOL. 17 · EMERGENCY PREPAREDNESS

The Complete Emergency First Aid Guide (Read This Before You Need It)

Eight emergencies, explained clearly enough to actually remember under pressure — CPR, choking, severe bleeding, burns, fractures, heatstroke, hypothermia, and severe allergic reactions.

19 MIN READ·UPDATED 2026·BASED ON STANDARD GLOBAL FIRST AID PROTOCOLS

Most people never think about first aid until the exact moment they desperately need it — and by then, it's too late to start reading. The handful of minutes before professional help arrives are usually the minutes that decide the outcome, whether that's on a hiking trail, at home, or in a restaurant.

This guide covers the emergencies where knowing what to do actually changes the outcome the most. None of it requires equipment you don't already have, and none of it takes more than a few minutes to learn well enough to remember.

Before you read on: this guide is for general awareness and is not a substitute for a certified first aid or CPR course. Techniques like chest compression depth and rate are easiest to learn correctly with hands-on practice. If you don't already hold a certification, a local Red Cross or equivalent course usually takes half a day and is worth far more than any article. In every scenario below, calling your local emergency number is step one, not a last resort.

01 — LIFE-THREATENING

CPR — Cardiac Arrest

WHEN SOMEONE COLLAPSES AND ISN'T BREATHING NORMALLY
Call emergency services immediately — or have someone else call while you start compressions. Every minute without CPR after cardiac arrest lowers survival chances significantly.
STEP BY STEP
1
Check responsiveness — tap their shoulders firmly and shout. If there's no response and they're not breathing normally (or only gasping), begin CPR.
2
Lay them flat on their back on a firm surface. Kneel beside their chest.
3
Place the heel of one hand on the center of the chest, between the nipples. Place your other hand on top, fingers interlaced.
4
Push hard and fast — at least 5 cm (2 inches) deep, at a rate of 100–120 compressions per minute. A good pace-setter: the beat of the song "Stayin' Alive" is almost exactly right.
5
Let the chest fully rise between compressions — don't lean on it. Keep going in cycles of 30 compressions until help arrives, the person starts breathing normally, or you're physically unable to continue.
6
If you're trained in rescue breaths and comfortable giving them, give 2 breaths after every 30 compressions. If not, hands-only CPR (compressions only, no breaths) is still highly effective and is what's now recommended for untrained bystanders.
Don't wait to be sure. Starting CPR on someone who didn't strictly need it causes far less harm than hesitating on someone who did.
02 — LIFE-THREATENING

Choking (The Heimlich Maneuver)

WHEN SOMEONE CAN'T BREATHE, COUGH, OR SPEAK
HOW TO TELL IT'S SERIOUS
  • They're clutching their throat, can't speak, cough, or make sound
  • Lips or face starting to turn blue
  • If they can still cough forcefully or speak, let them keep coughing — that airway isn't fully blocked yet
STEP BY STEP — ADULT OR CHILD OVER 1 YEAR
1
Stand behind the person, wrap your arms around their waist, and lean them slightly forward.
2
Make a fist with one hand and place the thumb side just above their belly button, well below the breastbone.
3
Grab your fist with your other hand and give a quick, sharp inward-and-upward thrust.
4
Repeat until the object is expelled or the person becomes unconscious.
5
If they become unconscious, lower them gently to the ground and begin CPR — chest compressions can also help dislodge the object. Call emergency services if you haven't already.
If you're alone and choking, you can perform abdominal thrusts on yourself using your own fist, or lean forward hard over any firm object like a chair back or counter edge.

03 — LIFE-THREATENING

Severe Bleeding

WHEN BLOOD IS SPURTING, POOLING, OR WON'T STOP
STEP BY STEP
1
Call emergency services if the bleeding is heavy or from a deep wound.
2
Apply firm, direct pressure to the wound using a clean cloth, gauze, or even your bare hand if nothing else is available.
3
Keep pressing — don't lift the cloth to check on it. If blood soaks through, add more layers on top rather than removing the first one.
4
If possible, raise the injured area above the level of the heart while continuing to apply pressure.
5
Once bleeding is under control, secure the dressing firmly with a bandage without cutting off circulation. Watch for numbness or color change in fingers/toes below the bandage.
Do not remove an object that's embedded in a wound (like glass or a knife) — pad and bandage around it instead, and let medical professionals remove it. Pulling it out can make bleeding significantly worse.
04 — COMMON EMERGENCY

Burns

HEAT, CHEMICAL, OR ELECTRICAL BURNS
STEP BY STEP — HEAT BURNS
1
Cool the burn under cool (not ice-cold) running water for 10–20 minutes. This is the single most effective thing you can do, and it works best in the first few minutes after the burn.
2
Remove any tight clothing or jewelry near the burn before swelling starts, unless it's stuck to the skin.
3
Cover loosely with a clean, non-fluffy cloth or cling film — don't wrap it tightly.
4
Seek medical care for any burn larger than the person's palm, any burn on the face/hands/genitals, or any burn that looks white, leathery, or charred.
Never apply butter, oil, toothpaste, or ice directly to a burn — all of these trap heat in or damage the skin further, despite being common myths.
05 — COMMON EMERGENCY

Suspected Fractures and Sprains

WHEN A LIMB IS DEFORMED, SWOLLEN, OR CAN'T BEAR WEIGHT
STEP BY STEP
1
Don't move the injured area unless it's necessary for safety. Don't try to straighten a visibly deformed limb.
2
Immobilize the area exactly as you found it — a rolled towel, magazine, or rigid object can act as a splint, secured with cloth or tape above and below the injury.
3
Apply an ice pack wrapped in cloth to reduce swelling — never directly on skin.
4
Elevate the limb if possible, and get medical attention — especially urgent if there's numbness, the skin has broken, or the limb looks visibly out of place.
06 — LIFE-THREATENING

Unconscious but Breathing

THE RECOVERY POSITION

If someone is unconscious but breathing normally and you've ruled out spinal injury (no fall from height, no traffic accident), putting them in the recovery position keeps their airway clear and prevents choking if they vomit.

STEP BY STEP
1
Kneel beside them. Place the arm nearest you at a right angle to their body, palm facing up.
2
Bring their far arm across their chest, and hold the back of their hand against the cheek closest to you.
3
With your other hand, grab the far knee and pull it up, keeping the foot flat on the ground.
4
Pull the knee toward you, rolling them onto their side. Tilt the head back slightly to keep the airway open, and check breathing continuously until help arrives.
07 — ENVIRONMENTAL EMERGENCY

Heatstroke and Hypothermia

OPPOSITE PROBLEMS, BOTH DANGEROUS
HEATSTROKE WARNING SIGNS
  • Body temperature above 40°C (104°F), hot and dry or flushed skin
  • Confusion, slurred speech, or loss of consciousness
  • Rapid pulse, headache, nausea

For heatstroke: call emergency services, move the person to shade or somewhere cool, remove excess clothing, and cool them rapidly — wet skin with cool water and fan them, or apply cold packs to the neck, armpits, and groin, where blood vessels sit close to the surface.

HYPOTHERMIA WARNING SIGNS
  • Uncontrollable shivering (or, in severe cases, shivering that has stopped)
  • Confusion, slurred speech, drowsiness
  • Slow, shallow breathing

For hypothermia: call emergency services, move the person somewhere warm and dry, remove wet clothing, and warm them gradually with blankets or dry layers — skin-to-skin contact under blankets works well. Warm (not hot) sweet drinks help if they're fully conscious and able to swallow safely.

Never apply direct high heat (like a hot bath or heating pad on bare skin) to someone with severe hypothermia — warming too fast can cause dangerous heart rhythm changes.

08 — LIFE-THREATENING

Severe Allergic Reaction (Anaphylaxis)

WHEN A REACTION SPREADS BEYOND THE SKIN
SIGNS IT'S ANAPHYLAXIS, NOT JUST A MILD REACTION
  • Swelling of the lips, tongue, throat, or face
  • Difficulty breathing, wheezing, or a tight throat
  • Widespread hives combined with dizziness or a rapid heartbeat
  • Sudden drop in blood pressure — pale skin, weakness, or collapse
STEP BY STEP
1
Call emergency services immediately — anaphylaxis can become fatal within minutes.
2
If the person has an epinephrine auto-injector (like an EpiPen), help them use it — or use it for them if they can't. Inject into the outer thigh, through clothing if needed.
3
Have them lie flat with legs elevated, unless they're having trouble breathing — in that case, let them sit up in whatever position is most comfortable for breathing.
4
If symptoms don't improve within 5–10 minutes and a second auto-injector is available, it can be given. Keep monitoring breathing and be ready to start CPR if they stop breathing.
